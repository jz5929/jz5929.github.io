TAMBURINS × MOLLY TEA

The Scent Menu

Your usual order. An unexpected perfume.

What if you could
wear your tea order?

That’s the idea behind The Scent Menu: a Tamburins × Molly Tea pop-up launching three new, drink-inspired fragrances. Guests try a small drink flight, meet its perfume counterparts, and leave with a new favorite.

I wanted the collection to move between floral, toasted, and fruity. Chinese jasmine tea, Korean roasted barley tea, and a mango-pomelo sago-inspired drink each give the collection a different starting point.

01 / THE FLORAL ORDER

Jasmine Cloud

Jasmine petals · Green tea · Soft musk

Inspired by jasmine tea and the creamy softness of a milk-tea finish. Airy and floral, with a clean tea note underneath.

The bottle: smoky glass and a folded silver cap, like a small sculpture on the tea counter.

At the pop-up: a jasmine milk-tea tasting, followed by the fragrance at a separate sampling station.

02 / THE SLOW-SIP ORDER

Roasted Hour

Toasted barley · Rice steam · Cedar

A comforting interpretation of Korean boricha, or roasted barley tea. Toasty grain notes, a little steam, and a quiet woody finish.

The bottle: pale blue glass with a rounded, stone-like cap. Cool on the outside, warm in character.

At the pop-up: a roasted barley-tea tasting created for this imagined collaboration, not an existing Molly Tea menu item.

03 / THE FRUITY ORDER

Pomelo Daydream

Pomelo peel · Ripe mango · Coconut milk

Inspired by a drinkable take on mango pomelo sago, the Hong Kong dessert. Bright citrus opens into soft mango and a creamy coconut accord.

The bottle: a speckled ivory egg with a little sculpted pomelo and leaf. The fruit detail is the first hint of what’s inside.

At the pop-up: a mini mango-pomelo sago drink, with a fresh citrus finish.

THE POP-UP / A PROPOSED WEEKEND IN NYC

Meet me at
the scent counter.

A café-sized space with a pale-blue drinks counter, brushed-metal perfume displays, and three menu cards. It should feel easy to wander into with a friend.

  1. Pick your order.

    Book a tasting slot or join the walk-in line. Choose floral, roasted, or fruity on a small menu card.

  2. Take a sip.

    Try the corresponding drink at the café counter. Each card names the drink and its cultural reference.

  3. Meet the scent.

    Move to the fragrance counter to try the perfume on a blotter. Staff explain how a flavor became a scent idea.

  4. Take a favorite home.

    Choose a proposed three-scent discovery set or a full-size bottle. Keep the menu card with a note of your favorite.

BEFORE / THE INVITATION

“What’s your usual?”

A three-part social series pairs each drink with its new perfume. Close-ups of jasmine, toasted grain, and pomelo lead into the bottle reveal, with an invitation to book a tasting.

DURING / THE MOMENT

One table. Three opinions.

Invite a small group of café, beauty, and food creators to try the flight together. Capture their different favorites, alongside the makers explaining the drink references.

AFTER / THE FOLLOW-UP

“Still thinking about the fruity one?”

An optional follow-up reminds guests which scent they chose and links to its proposed discovery set. No invented scarcity, just an easy way to find it again.

Did the idea travel
beyond the café?

I’d track tasting bookings, attendance, sampling-to-purchase conversion, and which scent guests choose. A later check would compare discovery-set purchases with full-size purchases. These are proposed measures, not campaign results.

03 / CARTIER
EVENT ANALYSIS

There’s a story behind the recap.

An event can start with one watch and end with a completely different purchase. That’s what made putting together the Roadster recap interesting.

The starting point

I brought reporting from the Roadster pop-ins into one national recap, pulling together sales, appointments, and boutique feedback. I wanted the presentation to explain how the events felt as well as how they performed.

How I approached it

I kept different reporting periods clearly labeled and put the figures alongside feedback on hospitality, product availability, and timing. Those details help explain what a sales total can’t tell you on its own.

The question I kept coming back to: what should we do differently next time?

EXCEL & MARKET ANALYSIS

Same brand.
Different shopping habits.

Chicago and Valley Fair each have their own mix of local shoppers and visitors. I explored how those differences could shape boutique planning.

Inside the spreadsheets

The workbooks bring together client segments, geographic mix, and comparisons with the wider market. Adjustable growth assumptions make it possible to explore how the mix might change.

What to keep in mind

A projection is a way to ask “what if?” I kept the assumptions visible so the numbers could support a planning conversation without pretending we knew exactly what would happen.
